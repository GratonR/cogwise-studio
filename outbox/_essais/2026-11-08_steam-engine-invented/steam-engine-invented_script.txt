Hot tea in a capped bottle crushes it as it cools. That's how the steam engine started.
Inside, the steam turns back into a few drops of water.
Steam takes over a thousand times more room, so almost nothing is left.
Now the air outside wins. It squeezes the bottle flat.
Air presses about one kilo on every square centimetre, from every side. Fourteen pounds per square inch.
Now use a metal tube with a piston, roped to a bucket.
Boil a little water underneath. Steam lifts the piston, and a latch holds it.
Take away the fire. The steam turns back to water and leaves a vacuum.
Release the latch. The air slams the piston down, and the bucket shoots up.
No steam pushed that bucket. The weight of the air did.
Denis Papin built this in 1690. Newcomen scaled it up to pump flooded mines.
Steam makes the vacuum. Air does the work. Like your bottle.
