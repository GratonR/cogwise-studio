At minus eighteen Celsius, zero Fahrenheit, your battery has lost half its punch. And your engine wants more.
Starting a car is a tug of war: power supplied versus power demanded.
A battery makes current by a chemical reaction, and cold slows it down.
At zero Celsius, thirty-two Fahrenheit, it gives about twenty percent less. At minus eighteen, nearly half.
Turn the key, and a small current wakes the starter's solenoid.
The solenoid pushes a tiny gear into the flywheel, then closes the big contact.
Now the starter motor gulps around four hundred and fifty amps.
The little gear spins ten to fifteen times for each engine turn, multiplying its force.
But cold oil turns thick, like honey, so the crankshaft fights back.
Less power in, more effort needed. That's the double squeeze.
A weak battery sags so low that the solenoid only clicks.
Once the engine fires, a one-way clutch lets go, and the little gear springs back.
Then the alternator refills the battery while you drive.
So cold weakens the battery, and thickens the oil it has to fight.
Tech tip: crank ten seconds max, then let the battery rest thirty seconds.
