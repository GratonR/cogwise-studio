Road salt doesn't actually melt ice. It needs water first.
The secret: ice almost always wears a thin film of liquid water.
The salt dissolves into that film and turns it into brine.
Salty water won't freeze at zero Celsius, thirty-two Fahrenheit. It stays liquid.
That brine melts the ice it touches, and drills down under each grain.
Melted ice is new water, so it dissolves more salt. A chain reaction.
But colder ice has a thinner film, and the salt dissolves slower and slower.
At minus one Celsius, thirty Fahrenheit, one kilo of salt melts forty-six kilos of ice within minutes.
At minus nine Celsius, fifteen Fahrenheit, it's barely six kilos. And it needs an hour.
Below minus twenty-one, minus six Fahrenheit, even the saltiest brine freezes solid.
So road crews switch to calcium chloride, or just throw sand for grip.
Salt doesn't melt ice. Salty water does.
