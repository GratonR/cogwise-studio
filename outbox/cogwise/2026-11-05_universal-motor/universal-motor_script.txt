See the blue sparks through your drill's vents? Two little blocks of carbon are making them.
They're carbon brushes, spring-loaded in their holders, feeding a series-wound universal motor.
The stator: two field windings around laminated steel poles, making a north and a south pole.
AC drives eddy currents through solid iron and heats it. Thin insulated sheets block them.
Inside spins the armature: a slotted, laminated core carrying copper coils.
Every coil ends on copper segments of the commutator, insulated from each other by mica.
Current enters through one brush, flows through the armature conductors, and leaves by the other.
The armature field sits at ninety degrees to the stator field. That's where torque peaks.
As it turns, the brushes ride onto the next segments, so the armature field never catches up.
Each commutation breaks the current in a coil. Its inductance fights back with an arc: the blue spark.
On AC, current flips a hundred times a second. In series, field and armature flip together.
With no load, the field weakens and speed soars past twenty thousand rpm.
All that arcing makes radio noise, so a suppression capacitor sits across the supply.
Series field, laminated iron, commutator and brushes: that's why it runs on AC or DC.
Tech tip: heavy sparking or a burning smell? Check brush length, and replace them in pairs.
