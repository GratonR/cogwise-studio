How does a door lock know this key is yours, and not your neighbor's?
The secret: tiny spring-loaded pins stop the lock from turning.
It all happens on one invisible line, between a turning part and a fixed part.
The plug is the cylinder your key turns, inside a fixed shell.
Each hole holds a stack: a key pin, a driver pin, and a spring.
With no key, springs push the drivers across the line. Jammed!
Your key's bumps and valleys lift each stack to a different height.
The right key puts every gap exactly on the line, all at once.
Nothing crosses the line, so the plug turns and pulls back the bolt.
Wrong key? One pin too high or too low, and nothing moves.
Surprise: ancient Egyptians used wooden pin locks about four thousand years ago!
Linus Yale Junior shrank it to pocket size in the 1860s.
Five or six pins with several heights give thousands of different keys.
So your key is a code: a row of heights only your lock understands.
Tech tip: stiff lock? Use graphite powder, not oil, which traps dust.
