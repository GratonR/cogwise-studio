The first working steam engine didn't run on steam. It ran on air.
The trick? Steam only fills the cylinder. Then you cool it.
It collapses into a few drops of water, leaving a vacuum.
Now the air outside shoves the piston down. Hard.
First, low-pressure steam from the boiler fills the cylinder as the piston rises.
Then a jet of cold water condenses the steam in an instant.
Nothing is left underneath, so the air slams the piston down and rocks a giant wooden beam.
The other end of the beam lifts a pump rod and drags water out of a flooded mine.
A puddle of water on top of the piston seals the gap, so the air can't sneak under.
Air presses about one kilo on every square centimetre: on this piston, two tonnes. Forty-four hundred pounds.
Twelve strokes a minute, each one lifting about forty-five litres. Twelve gallons.
Newcomen, 1712, near Dudley Castle. Steam makes the vacuum. Air does the work.
